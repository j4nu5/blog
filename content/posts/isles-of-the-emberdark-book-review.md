_Isles of the Emberdark_ is the fifth book in the _Secret Projects_ series from
Brandon Sanderson. It is set within the _Cosmere_ universe, and is, strictly
speaking, a sequel to _Sixth of the Dusk_. Reading _Sixth of the Dusk_, however,
is not a prerequisite, and _Isles of the Emberdark_ can be read as a standalone
novel. The first few chapters in _Isles of the Emberdark_ cover the plot of
_Sixth of the Dusk_ as a series of flashbacks.

The rest of this review assumes a basic familiarity with the Cosmere's magic
system and technical concepts.

## Overview

- Pace: Fast-paced
- Plot or Character Driven?: Mostly plot-driven
- Major themes: Colonialism, Coming of age, Tradition vs. Progress
- Prose: Typical Brandon Sanderson "clear windowpane" style prose
- Links to other reviews: [StoryGraph](https://app.thestorygraph.com/books/f2045e51-89df-46b7-9100-7288b9efd46e), [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/210300489-isles-of-the-emberdark)

## Plot Summary

_Isles of the Emberdark_ marks the first novel where the Cosmere is clearly
switching genres from Fantasy to Sci-Fi. It is written from the point of view of
two main characters - Sixth of the Dusk, a bird trapper from the fantastical
world of First of the Sun (Drominad) struggling to find relevance in the modern
world, and Starling, a young dragon trying to find her place as the captain of
a merchant spaceship. Their stories collide on an island near First of the Sun's
perpendicularity, where a group of Malwish Scadrians are trying to find
Drominad's perpendicularity in Shadesmar, just like Starling, after being tipped
off about its location by a group of Iriali. Dusk, who is trying to find a
bargaining chip in his planet's negotiations with the Malwish (The Ones from
Above), reaches this island after traveling from the actual Drominad
perpendicularity and discovering along the way that he has the ability to
navigate in the Emberdark - the nothingness between planets in Shadesmar.

All of the involved parties discover the presence of a Threnodite entity in the
center of this island. Dusk believes that this entity is the legendary _Dakwara_
that his people's lore talks about. The Malwish and Starling dismiss his beliefs
as lore of a primitive people without realizing that the Cognitive Realm is
shaped by the thoughts of people in the Physical Realm, and hence if an entire
group of people believe in the Dakwara, the Threnodite entity will _become_ the
Dakwara, and follow the rules of the lore of Dusk's people.

Dusk eventually defeats the Malwish on the island, Starling consolidates her
position as a captain, and First of the Sun - which now has a Threnodite entity
protecting its perpendicularity - starts advertising its navigation services for
hire to the wider Cosmere.

## Analysis

### Colonialism

First of the Sun is a backwater planet at the edges of the Cosmere in the
Physical Realm. In the Cognitive Realm, however, it happens to sit somewhere
between Scadrial and Roshar, thereby making it a logistical chokepoint in the
war between Scadrial and Roshar.

Scadrial is trying to colonize Drominad with the same mindset as that of any
empire - total domination, complete erasure of local culture, and eventual
assimilation.

I couldn't help but think of my own country's (India's) colonization by Britain,
and the complete obliteration of India's economy that followed it.

Fortunately for Drominad though, they were able to recognize their colonization
before it occurred, and were able to find a bargaining chip in time.

### Tradition vs. Progress

This was the theme of Dusk's arc, along with his struggle to find his identity
in a world which was changing more rapidly than he would have liked. He is able
to grow, though, with some help from Vathi.

> So he didn’t look back. Looking back was for people who regretted what they
> were about to do. Instead, he kept paddling. One man. Two birds. Three kinds
> of infinity. And an entire world that, remarkably, still needed him.

### Prose

I didn't like Starling's parts in the book. She is supposed to be a young dragon
and her point-of-view chapters' childish prose was a stark contrast to Dusk's
deep inner monologue.

I found her character arc too predictable, and her chapters' prose too thin.
I usually don't mind Sanderson's "clear windowpane" style prose, but in this
case it was constantly preventing my immersion into the story, especially when
it was getting contrasted with Dusk's point-of-view chapters.

## Conclusion

The ending was quite satisfying and saved the book for me. While it was not a
full-blown Sanderlanche, it was an enjoyable action-thriller-style climax.

3.75/5 stars.
